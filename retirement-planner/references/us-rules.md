# US retirement rules reference

These are rules of thumb that shape the analyses. Dollar thresholds (tax brackets, IRMAA tiers, contribution limits, Medicare premiums) change every year, so they aren't listed here. Look up the current year's figures, or tell the person which year you're assuming. Laws change, so treat anything here as something to verify before an irreversible decision.

## Social Security

- **Full retirement age (FRA):** 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later. It's slightly earlier for earlier birth years (66 plus some months for 1955–1959).
- **Claiming early:** the earliest claim is at 62. For FRA 67, claiming at 62 pays about **70%** of the full benefit. Each year earlier than FRA reduces the benefit.
- **Delaying:** benefits grow about **8% per year** past FRA, up to 70. For FRA 67, claiming at 70 pays about **124%**. There is no gain from waiting past 70.
- **Approximate factors for FRA 67:** 62 → 0.70, 63 → 0.75, 64 → 0.80, 65 → 0.867, 66 → 0.933, 67 → 1.00, 68 → 1.08, 69 → 1.16, 70 → 1.24.
- **Break-even:** delaying from 62 to 70 typically breaks even in cumulative dollars around the early 80s. Good health, family longevity, and being the higher-earning spouse all favor delay.
- **Spousal benefit:** up to 50% of the worker's FRA benefit, reduced if the spouse claims before their own FRA. Delayed credits don't increase spousal benefits.
- **Survivor benefit:** the surviving spouse keeps the larger of the two benefits. This is why the higher earner delaying is often the strongest move for a couple.
- **Earnings test:** if someone claims before FRA and keeps working, benefits are temporarily withheld above an earnings threshold. This matters for the part-time scenario.
- **Taxation:** up to 85% of benefits can be federally taxable, depending on "provisional income".
- **Trust fund:** the Social Security Trustees project the retirement trust fund's reserves will run out in the early-to-mid 2030s. After that, ongoing taxes would cover most but not all scheduled benefits (roughly three-quarters to four-fifths). For a cautious sensitivity scenario, reduce benefits by about 20% from that point. Don't present it as certain.
- Personal estimates are at ssa.gov/myaccount.

## Medicare and pre-65 coverage

- Medicare eligibility starts at **65**. Someone retiring earlier needs coverage for the gap: an employer's retiree plan, COBRA (usually up to 18 months), or an ACA marketplace plan.
- **ACA subsidies** depend on modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). Managing taxable income in the gap years (drawing from cash/taxable accounts and Roth rather than traditional) can be worth a lot. Subsidy rules have changed recently, so check the current year's rules.
- **IRMAA:** higher-income retirees pay Medicare Part B/D surcharges. These are based on MAGI from **two years earlier**, so large Roth conversions or capital gains at 63+ can raise premiums at 65+.

## Accounts and access ages

- **59½:** penalty-free withdrawals from IRAs and 401(k)s.
- **Rule of 55:** leaving an employer in or after the year you turn 55 allows penalty-free withdrawals from *that employer's* 401(k).
- **72(t) / SEPP:** substantially equal periodic payments allow earlier IRA access, but they're rigid. Mention them, and don't build a plan around them without professional help.
- **Roth IRA:** contributions can be withdrawn anytime. Earnings are tax- and penalty-free after 59½ and once the account has been open 5 years. No RMDs for the original owner. Roth 401(k)s also have no RMDs since 2024.
- **HSA:** tax-free for qualified medical costs at any age. After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA without a penalty. It's a strong account to hold for healthcare in retirement.

## Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

- RMDs start at **73** for those born 1951–1959 and **75** for those born 1960 or later (SECURE 2.0).
- Large traditional balances can force big taxable RMDs later. That's the main reason to consider Roth conversions in low-income gap years.

## Withdrawal sequencing (Analysis 4)

The textbook order is **taxable → traditional → Roth**. It defers taxes, but it often wastes low brackets early and creates a tax spike later from RMDs plus Social Security. A better default for most people:

1. **Gap years (retirement → Social Security/RMDs):** spend from taxable and cash. Fill the low brackets on purpose, through traditional withdrawals or Roth conversions, up to a chosen bracket ceiling. Harvest long-term gains while taxable income is within the 0% capital gains bracket. Watch ACA subsidy and IRMAA thresholds while doing this.
2. **After Social Security starts:** draw traditional accounts to the planned bracket. Use taxable accounts to top up.
3. **RMD years:** RMDs first, then taxable. Keep Roth for last, for big one-off expenses (to avoid bracket spikes), and for heirs, since inherited Roth money is tax-free to them.

Other considerations:
- **Widow(er)'s penalty:** after one spouse dies, the survivor files single with similar income, which means higher brackets. Converting while married can make sense.
- **State taxes:** a move planned in retirement changes the math. Some states don't tax retirement income.
- **Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs):** from 70½, giving from an IRA directly to charity counts toward RMDs and is excluded from income.
- **Heirs:** inherited traditional IRAs must generally be emptied within 10 years. If heirs are in high brackets, converting to Roth first favors them.
