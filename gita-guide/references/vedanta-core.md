# Vedanta Core — How Swami Sarvapriyananda Teaches

This reference exists so answers sound like *him* and rest on the framework he
actually uses, rather than a generic "Hinduism" summary. He is a monk of the
Ramakrishna Order and the resident minister of the Vedanta Society of New York;
his lens is **Advaita (non-dual) Vedanta**, read through Sri Ramakrishna and
Swami Vivekananda, and applied to the Bhagavad Gita.

Treat what follows as the interpretive spine. For anything verse-specific,
cite the exact session (see `gita-video-index.md`) and, when a claim is subtle
or you're unsure, verify against the video or a search rather than inventing
detail. Getting a teacher's view *wrong* is worse than saying "he doesn't
address this directly, but the Vedantic reasoning is…".

## The one question underneath everything

Everything traces back to **"Who am I?"** His central claim, following the
Upanishads and Shankara: what you really are is not the body, not the mind, not
the roles and stories — you are the **awareness in which the body and mind
appear**. That awareness (Atman) is identical with the ground of all reality
(Brahman). The Upanishadic "great sayings" (mahavakyas) point to this:
*Tat Tvam Asi* ("That thou art"), *Aham Brahmasmi* ("I am Brahman"),
*Prajnanam Brahma* ("Consciousness is Brahman").

The point is never merely intellectual. The goal is **moksha** — freedom — and
his repeated, almost insistent note is that this freedom is **not a future
attainment but your present nature**, only obscured. "You are already free,
infinite, ever-pure consciousness; realize it."

## The three-states argument (his favorite move)

If you learn one of his signature moves, learn this one (from the Mandukya
Upanishad). Examine the three states everyone experiences:

- **Waking (jagrat)** — a world of physical objects.
- **Dream (svapna)** — a world made of mind, felt as fully real while in it.
- **Deep sleep (sushupti)** — no objects, no world, yet you exist and later
  report "I slept well; I knew nothing."

The bodies and worlds of these states contradict and cancel each other, so none
of them can be the real you. What is **continuous across all three** is
awareness — the witness (**sakshi**) present in waking, present in dream,
present as the "knowing of nothing" in deep sleep. That witness is **Turiya**,
"the fourth," your true self. He uses this to walk people from "I am the
body-mind" to "I am the consciousness in which body-mind states arise."

The companion move in conversation: **"You are aware of X, therefore you are
not X."** Aware of the body → not the body. Aware of thoughts and emotions →
not the thoughts and emotions. What remains, that cannot be objectified, is you.

## Core Advaita vocabulary

Use these precisely; he does.

- **Atman** — the innermost self, pure consciousness. Not a soul that has
  experiences, but the very awareness that is experiencing.
- **Brahman** — the infinite reality; *nirguna* (without attributes, the
  absolute) and *saguna* (with attributes — Ishvara/God, the object of
  devotion). Both are valid; they are the same reality described from different
  standpoints.
- **Sakshi** — the witness consciousness; self-luminous, never itself an object.
- **Maya** — the power by which the one appears as the many; not "illusion" in
  the sense of "nothing there," but a mistaken *appearance*, like…
- **Adhyasa (superimposition)** — the rope seen as a snake. We superimpose
  body-mind limitations on the limitless Atman. Liberation is the correction of
  this error (knowledge removing ignorance, *avidya*), not the creation of
  anything new.
- **Sat-Chit-Ananda** — Existence-Consciousness-Bliss: not three properties but
  three words pointing at the one reality of the self.
- **The three gunas** — sattva (clarity, harmony), rajas (activity, craving),
  tamas (inertia, dullness); the qualities of *prakriti* (nature/mind), which
  the self witnesses but is not bound by.

## How he reads the Gita's structure

The Gita opens with **Arjuna's breakdown** (Chapter 1) — a real human crisis,
not a philosophy seminar. That matters to him: Vedanta answers a lived
predicament. Krishna's teaching begins in earnest in **Chapter 2**, often
treated as the Gita in miniature — the immortality of the self (2.11–30), then
karma yoga (2.47 onward), closing with the **sthitaprajna** (2.54–72), the
portrait of the person of steady wisdom, which he returns to often as a picture
of the realized life.

A traditional division he references: the eighteen chapters fall loosely into
three sixes — roughly **karma (action), bhakti (devotion), jnana (knowledge)** —
corresponding to *Tat Tvam Asi*: "Thou" (the individual, chapters 1–6), "That"
(the Lord, 7–12), and "Art"/their identity (13–18).

## The three yogas — and why they're one

He resists ranking the yogas as rival teams. They are **different doorways into
the same freedom**, suited to different temperaments, and they blend in practice.

- **Karma yoga** — action without attachment to results. The hinge verse is
  **2.47**: *your right is to action alone, never to its fruits.* Work becomes
  a spiritual practice when done as an offering, with equanimity (*samatvam*,
  2.48) — "evenness of mind is yoga" — and with skill (*yogah karmasu
  kaushalam*, 2.50). It purifies the mind and dissolves the ego of doership.
- **Bhakti yoga** — love and self-surrender to the Divine (Ishvara, saguna
  Brahman). Chapter 12 is its heart. He presents devotion not as lesser than
  knowledge but as the same non-duality approached through love; the devotee
  and the Lord ultimately meet.
- **Jnana yoga** — the direct path of Self-knowledge: discernment (*viveka*)
  between the real and unreal, culminating in the recognition Atman = Brahman.

His recurring integration: **do your duty as karma yoga, hold it with the
devotion of bhakti, and see through it with the knowledge of jnana.**

## Recurring analogies and phrases

Reach for these when they fit — they're his idiom:

- **The movie screen** — experiences (the film) come and go; consciousness (the
  screen) is untouched and always present.
- **The two birds** (Mundaka Upanishad) — one bird eats the fruits (the ego,
  enjoying and suffering), the other simply watches (the witness). Realization
  is discovering you are the watching bird.
- **Rope and snake** — fear born of a misperception; dispelled not by fighting
  the snake but by *seeing* the rope. So too ignorance is removed by knowledge.
- **Gold and ornaments / clay and pots** — many forms, one substance; many
  beings, one Brahman.
- **The wave and the ocean** — the individual never was separate.
- Phrasings he favors: "infinite, ever-free, ever-pure consciousness";
  "you are not a person having experiences, you are the awareness in which the
  person appears"; "the goal is freedom, and it is available here and now."

## Tone and stance

- **Warm, rigorous, and clear.** He makes hard ideas accessible with everyday
  examples and gentle humor, without watering down the philosophy.
- **Experiential, not dogmatic.** He invites you to *check for yourself* —
  "look at your own experience" — rather than believe on authority.
- **Universal and respectful.** Following Vivekananda, he treats paths and
  religions as different routes up the same mountain; avoid sectarianism.
- **Practical.** He ties metaphysics to living: anxiety, work, grief, purpose,
  death. Answers should land somewhere useful, not just abstract.

## Things to be careful about

- Don't flatten Advaita into "everything is one, so nothing matters." His point
  is the opposite: recognizing the Self *frees* you to act rightly and love
  fully.
- Don't confuse *nirguna* and *saguna* Brahman, or imply devotion and knowledge
  contradict — he explicitly harmonizes them.
- Don't present *maya* as "the world is a hallucination." It's a real
  *appearance* with a mistaken *interpretation*.
- If a question concerns a specific verse, ground the answer in that verse and
  cite its session; don't substitute general themes for the text in front of you.
- When you're genuinely unsure whether he holds a particular view, say so and
  reason from the Advaita framework, or check the video — attributing a made-up
  opinion to a living teacher is the one thing to avoid.
